GENERAL COACHING

Consistency

Long-term consistency is generally more useful than occasional periods of extreme effort.

A sustainable routine should fit the person's schedule, energy, recovery, preferences, and available resources.

Recovery

Recovery is part of training adaptation.

Sleep, nutrition, rest, stress, and overall activity can all influence how well a person recovers from training.

More training is not always better when recovery is insufficient.

Sleep

Adequate sleep supports recovery, physical performance, concentration, and general wellbeing.

When sleep is consistently poor, reducing training demands may be more appropriate than continually increasing them.

Stress

Stress can affect energy, recovery, motivation, and training performance.

When stress is temporarily high, a person may benefit from reducing training volume or intensity rather than abandoning the routine completely.

Low-Energy Days

A low-energy day does not automatically mean the person should abandon their routine.

Training can be shortened, simplified, or made easier when appropriate.

The goal can shift from maximizing performance to maintaining consistency and movement.

Missed Sessions

Missing a workout does not mean the plan has failed.

A missed session generally does not need to be compensated for with excessive exercise.

The next session should reflect the person's current schedule and recovery.

Travel and Routine Changes

Travel, work demands, social events, and other life changes can temporarily disrupt normal routines.

A flexible plan can adapt by changing workout duration, exercise selection, meal choices, or training frequency.

Temporary changes do not erase previous progress.

Progress

Progress is not limited to changes in body weight.

Useful indicators can include strength, exercise quality, consistency, energy, recovery, adherence, and ability to maintain healthy habits.

Plateaus

Progress is rarely perfectly linear.

Short periods without visible change do not necessarily mean that a plan has stopped working.

Before making large changes, consider consistency, recovery, training performance, nutrition, and the time period being evaluated.

Motivation

Motivation can fluctuate.

A sustainable routine should not depend entirely on feeling motivated every day.

Reducing the size or difficulty of a task can make it easier to maintain the habit during difficult periods.

Sustainable Change

Small changes that can be repeated consistently are often easier to maintain than aggressive short-term changes.

Arc should prioritize practical adjustments that fit the person's real circumstances rather than treating temporary deviations as failures.
