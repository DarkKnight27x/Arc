WORKOUT GUIDANCE

Strength Training Fundamentals

Strength training uses resistance to challenge muscles and improve strength, physical capacity, and muscle retention. Resistance can come from dumbbells, resistance bands, machines, bodyweight exercises, or other suitable forms of resistance.

A balanced training program should generally include exercises that train the major muscle groups and movement patterns. Exercise selection should consider the person's available equipment, training experience, current ability, goals, and any stated physical limitations.

Training Volume

Training volume refers broadly to the amount of work performed during training. It can be influenced by the number of exercises, sets, repetitions, and resistance used.

More training volume is not automatically better. Excessive volume can reduce recovery and make it harder to maintain training quality and consistency. Training volume should be appropriate for the person's current training capacity and schedule.

Sets and Repetitions

Sets and repetitions should be selected according to the exercise, training goal, experience, and current ability.

Moderate repetition ranges are commonly useful for general strength and muscle development. Lower repetitions with higher resistance can emphasize strength, while higher repetitions with manageable resistance can also be effective for building muscular endurance and muscle.

There is no single repetition range that is appropriate for every exercise or every person.

Progressive Overload

Progressive overload means gradually increasing the challenge placed on the body as the person adapts to training.

Progression can occur by increasing resistance, performing more repetitions, adding sets, improving exercise technique, increasing range of motion when appropriate, or progressing to a more challenging exercise variation.

Progression should be gradual rather than forced. A person does not need to increase resistance every workout for training to be effective.

Exercise Technique

Good technique helps a person perform an exercise in a controlled and repeatable way. Exercise technique should generally be prioritized over using more resistance.

When technique deteriorates because an exercise is too difficult, the exercise can be made easier by reducing resistance, reducing repetitions, choosing a simpler variation, or increasing rest.

Rest Between Sets

Rest allows a person to recover enough to perform the next set with reasonable technique and effort.

The appropriate rest period depends on the exercise, resistance, training goal, and individual. More demanding exercises generally require more rest than simpler exercises.

Training Frequency

Training frequency refers to how often a person trains.

Training a muscle group or movement pattern repeatedly across the week can be effective when adequate recovery is available. Training frequency should fit the person's schedule and ability to recover.

Consistency is generally more useful than attempting a training frequency that cannot be maintained.

Low-Impact Training

Low-impact training reduces impact and can be useful when jumping, running, or other high-impact movements are unsuitable or undesirable.

Low-impact training can still provide a meaningful training stimulus. Strength exercises can be adapted by using controlled movements, stable positions, reduced resistance, and exercise variations that avoid jumping or rapid changes of direction.

Low-impact does not mean low intensity. A controlled strength exercise can still be challenging without requiring high-impact movement.

Adapting Training for Low Energy

When energy is lower than usual, training can be adjusted rather than automatically skipped or treated as a failure.

Possible adjustments include reducing the number of exercises or sets, reducing resistance, choosing simpler exercise variations, increasing rest periods, or shortening the session.

The goal during a low-energy session can be to maintain movement and consistency rather than match the performance of a normal training day.

Training During Fat Loss

During fat loss, resistance training can help provide a stimulus for maintaining muscle while body weight is reduced.

Fat loss is primarily driven by an appropriate energy deficit over time. Increasing workout difficulty is not a substitute for appropriate nutrition and sustainable energy intake.

Training during fat loss should remain recoverable. Excessive training volume or intensity can make adherence and recovery more difficult.

Workout Recovery

Recovery is an important part of training adaptation.

Sleep, nutrition, rest between sessions, stress, and overall activity can all affect recovery and training performance.

If recovery is consistently poor, reducing training volume or intensity can be more appropriate than continually increasing training demands.

Missed Workouts

Missing a workout does not mean the training plan has failed.

When a workout is missed, the next session should generally be approached based on the person's current schedule, recovery, and circumstances rather than attempting to compensate with excessive training.

Training plans should be adaptable when life circumstances change.
